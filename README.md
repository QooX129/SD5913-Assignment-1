## Designers and Artists
Designers and artists share a common foundation: both begin with an idea and use creative decisions to transform that idea into a material or digital form. At the center of both practices is communication between the creator and other people. Designers and artists use their work to convey messages, experiences, emotions, and ways of understanding the world. This people-based approach is also reflected in the Design Council's (n.d.) principles of putting people first and communicating visually and inclusively. They both require imagination, technical skill, observation, and an understanding of how their work may be experienced by others. However, their methods and intentions can differ. A designer generally communicates a message or solves a practical problem for a particular audience, client, or user, while an artist more often communicates personal ideas, emotions, or interpretations of the world. Although the two roles can overlap, design is usually guided by function and specific requirements, whereas art allows greater emphasis on personal expression and open interpretation. For example, Tate (n.d.) describes generative art as computer-based art made through predetermined systems, showing how artistic communication can also involve rules, technology, and audience participation.
## Why Programming 
Programming connects closely with both design and art because it enables ideas to be developed into interactive and functional digital experiences. Beattie (2020) presents code as an expressive medium, suggesting that this practice involves creative choices about structure, interaction, and the experience produced for other people. It gives creators another way to communicate with people by allowing an audience to interact with, influence, and respond to the work. For designers, programming provides greater control over structure, behavior, accessibility, and the way users interact with a product. For artists, it can serve as a creative medium through which they explore movement, sound, images, systems, and participation. Programming is important because it allows creators to translate concepts into precise instructions. As Lovelace (1843) argues, a machine can execute what people know how to order it to perform, but it does not originate ideas independently. Programming also allows creators to test and revise their work in response to users or changing conditions, an approach consistent with the Agile Alliance's (n.d.) emphasis on working software, changing requirements, and regular reflection. Therefore, programming is not only a technical skill; it is also a means of extending creative communication and strengthening the relationship between an idea, its creator, and its audience.
